One page, read slowly

  1. 01

    It starts as a book.

    A physics textbook — the one every pupil in terminale carries.

  2. 02

    Open it, and it asks a question.

    At what angle does a thrown ball land furthest? On paper, you would take the answer on trust.

  3. 03

    The figure arrives, and it is live.

    Axes, like any textbook's. But it runs the lesson's own equations.

    y = x·tan θ − g·x² / (2v₀²·cos²θ)
  4. 04

    It works the problem out, point by point.

    Thrown at 30°, every position is computed from the lesson's own equation.

    θ = 30° → R = 14.2 m
  5. 05

    Then 60° — and it lands in the same place.

    Not a coincidence: sin 2θ is the same for both. The figure found that; nobody drew it.

    θ = 60° → R = 14.2 m
  6. 06

    45° goes furthest. That is the answer.

    A textbook that does the mathematics while you read it. That is the whole idea.

Chapter 4 · Motion in a field

The furthest throw

Exercise 3. A ball leaves the ground at 12.7 m/s. At what angle does it land furthest?

  1. x(t) = v₀ cos θ · t
  2. y(t) = v₀ sin θ · t − ½ g t²
  3. y = 0 ⇒ t = 2 v₀ sin θ / g
  4. R = v₀² · sin 2θ / g
  5. sin 2θ max at 2θ = 90° ⇒ θ = 45°

The proof, right here

Drag the mirror.

The reflected ray is not drawn. It is solved on every movement by r = i − 2(i·n)n — the same few lines that run inside the books. The angle in equals the angle out because the geometry says so, not because someone drew it to look right.
